The Electromagnetic Animal

We are electrical organisms living inside a planetary electromagnetic environment. That does not prove Wi-Fi is poisoning us, or that consciousness is an electromagnetic field. But it also makes “electromagnetic fields have absolutely no effect on us” the wrong starting point.

Thomas Stephens · September 2026 · living notes

Start with the obvious: fields interact with bodies

The nervous system works through electrochemical gradients and changing membrane voltages. The heart is electrically active. Light is electromagnetic radiation. MRI, radio, microwaves, power lines and cell phones all occupy different parts of the electromagnetic spectrum.

So the useful question is not whether electromagnetic fields interact with living matter. They do. The useful questions are which frequencies, at what intensity, at what distance, for how long, through what mechanism, and with what biological consequence?

Low frequency

Electric and magnetic fields can induce voltages and tiny currents in conductive tissue. At sufficiently high intensity they can stimulate nerves and muscles.

Radiofrequency

RF energy can be absorbed by tissue. At high enough exposure, the established effect is heating. Modern exposure limits are built around preventing established harmful effects.

Ionizing radiation

X-rays and still-higher-energy radiation carry enough energy per photon to damage molecules and DNA directly. Cell-phone RF is far below this ionizing range.

Biological effect is not the same thing as health hazard. A measurable interaction can be harmless, beneficial, harmful, or too small to matter clinically. That distinction is central to the World Health Organization's treatment of EMF.

The phone in your hand is different from the tower on the hill

A cell phone has to transmit back to the network. Its radio is adaptive: when conditions are poor, the device can raise transmission power to maintain the link. When the phone is pressed against the head or carried against the body, the source is only millimeters away.

The National Cancer Institute notes that the body's absorbed RF dose drops quickly as distance from the phone increases, and that weak signal can cause a phone to boost its RF transmission power. Speakerphone, a headset, or simply moving the phone away from the body can therefore reduce personal exposure substantially.

Phone against head: nearby tissue receives the highest exposure from the handset. Phone on a table: even modest separation dramatically reduces absorbed exposure. Weak network signal: the phone may transmit harder to reach the network. Bluetooth: usually transmits at much lower power than the cellular radio.

That is why saying “cell towers are powerful” and saying “my handset is an important exposure source” are not contradictory. A tower may transmit vastly more total power, but distance, antenna geometry and the body's proximity to the source matter enormously.

But this still does not establish that normal phone use is harming us

The strongest public-health evidence remains reassuring. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration says the weight of scientific evidence has not linked cell-phone RF with health problems. The National Cancer Institute likewise says evidence to date suggests cell-phone use does not cause brain or other cancers in humans.

That conclusion is compatible with continued research. Science rarely proves an absolute negative; it asks whether a proposed effect is detectable, reproducible, dose-dependent and large enough to matter. Small or unusual effects can be difficult to establish, especially as wireless technologies and exposure patterns evolve.

The scientifically careful position is neither “RF does nothing” nor “RF is secretly poisoning everyone.” It is: RF interacts with the body; established hazards occur at sufficiently high exposure; ordinary regulated exposures have not been shown to produce the broad health harms often claimed; open questions remain worth studying.

Weak magnetic fields can still matter to biology

This is the part that makes the subject genuinely strange.

Animals across many branches of life can detect Earth's magnetic field for orientation or navigation. One major hypothesis involves cryptochrome, a light-sensitive protein capable of forming short-lived radical pairs whose electron-spin chemistry can respond to magnetic fields.

A 2024 Nature Communications paper modeled how a tightly bound radical pair in cryptochrome could remain sensitive to an Earth-strength field of roughly 50 microtesla through an asymmetric reaction pathway involving the quantum Zeno effect. This does not prove that human consciousness is magnetic, but it does demonstrate something important: biology can contain mechanisms that amplify extremely weak physical influences into usable chemical information.

Photon → activates a molecule Electron transfer → creates a radical pair Magnetic field → shifts spin-dependent reaction probabilities Chemistry → becomes a biological signal

And humans may not be magnetically blind

In 2019, researchers at Caltech and collaborators reported a repeatable change in human EEG alpha activity when participants were exposed, under controlled blinded conditions, to certain rotations of an Earth-strength magnetic field. The response depended on the direction and polarity of the field.

The study did not show that people consciously know where magnetic north is, and it did not establish a practical human magnetic sense. It showed a measurable neural response under specific laboratory conditions. The authors argued that the polarity dependence did not fit a simple cryptochrome compass and left a ferromagnetic mechanism, such as biological magnetite, as a possibility.

This is interesting evidence, not a finished story. Human magnetoreception remains an unsettled research area. A neural response is not automatically conscious perception, and one experiment does not define what the mechanism is for everyday human life.

Mind, body, spirit

Science can measure fields, currents, tissue heating, molecular reactions, neural firing and EEG rhythms. It can ask whether a stimulus changes behavior, sleep, cognition, disease risk or physiology.

“Spirit” is harder. There is no agreed scientific instrument for measuring a soul, chakra, aura, life force or spiritual field. That means science has not demonstrated that RF changes the spirit — but it also means a spiritual claim cannot simply be settled by measuring SAR or running an EEG.

My own philosophical interest begins where the measurement ends: a human being is not an electrically isolated object. We are living electrochemical systems, bathed from birth in the Sun's radiation, Earth's electric and magnetic environment, atmospheric electricity and now a dense layer of human-made signals. Whether any of that has a meaningful spiritual dimension is an open philosophical question, not an established biomedical fact.

A sane precautionary approach

You do not have to believe cell phones are dangerous to choose low-cost ways of reducing unnecessary exposure. Distance is powerful and costs almost nothing.

Use speakerphone or a headset for long calls when convenient. Do not press the phone against your body when it does not need to be there. If reception is extremely poor, waiting for a better signal can reduce how hard the phone needs to transmit. Avoid fear-based products and claims that promise magical “EMF protection” without credible measurements or evidence.

These are exposure-reduction choices, not treatment recommendations and not evidence that ordinary wireless use is unsafe.

The part I want to keep

I think we lose something when this subject gets divided into two camps: one saying electromagnetic fields are obviously destroying us, the other acting as if biological sensitivity to electromagnetic phenomena is ridiculous.

Nature is subtler than either story.

We already know that fields can stimulate neurons, heat tissue, interfere with electronics, guide animals and alter spin-dependent chemistry. We also have decades of human exposure data that have not established the sweeping harms sometimes attributed to ordinary wireless technology.

The interesting position is curiosity with calibration: take the physics seriously, take the biology seriously, take uncertainty seriously — and leave room for wonder without turning wonder into proof.
