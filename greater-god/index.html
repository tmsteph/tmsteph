The Greater God

What if the Christian creator God, Satan, Eden, the fall, and the struggle between good and evil are all parts of a reality larger than the categories we use to describe them?

Thomas Stephens · September 2026

A thought, not a doctrine

This is not an attempt to replace Christianity, Hinduism, Buddhism, Taoism, or any other tradition with a neat universal theory. It is a personal attempt to notice a pattern that seems to appear across them.

I have been wondering whether the God we describe in human language may be one expression of a still greater divine reality: a source large enough to contain creation, opposition, freedom, suffering, transformation, and reunion without itself being reduced to any one of those things.

Maybe the deepest story is not simply God versus Satan. Maybe both the creator and the adversary exist inside a reality whose final purpose neither side can overturn.

Why would God allow an adversary?

Traditional Christianity usually does not say that God created Satan as evil. Satan is understood as a created spiritual being who rebels. Evil is therefore not a second ultimate power equal to God.

But the harder question remains: if God is sovereign, why permit the rebellion at all?

One answer found within Christian thought is that freedom, love, courage, mercy, forgiveness, and redemption may require a world in which rejection is actually possible. God can permit an evil act without calling the evil itself good, while still drawing something greater out of the history that follows.

That makes Satan strangely important to the story without making Satan equal to God. The adversary opposes, tempts, divides, and accuses. Yet even that opposition can become part of the setting in which beings learn what they love and what they choose.

Eden and the birth of duality

The Genesis story centers on the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil. The human beings begin in a garden, naked and apparently unashamed. After eating the fruit, they suddenly perceive themselves differently. They see nakedness, separation, danger, responsibility, labor, mortality, and moral distinction.

It is tempting to read this not only as a rule being broken, but as a mythic description of consciousness changing state.

The text never identifies the fruit. The idea that it was psilocybin or another psychedelic is speculative and has no established textual or archaeological basis. Still, psychedelic experience can serve as a modern metaphor for the story because it can involve radical changes in self-perception, unity, fear, meaning, and the boundaries of the ego.

The deeper point for me is not the chemistry. It is the transition:

Unity and innocence Knowledge and separation Struggle with good and evil Wisdom through experience Conscious reunion

Perhaps humanity did not merely fall away from paradise. Perhaps we entered the dangerous process of becoming consciously capable of returning to it.

Christianity already contains a hint of this

Christianity begins with a garden, but the Bible does not end with humanity simply being placed back into prehistoric Eden. Revelation ends with the New Jerusalem: a city, a river, nations, gates, culture, and the Tree of Life.

That is a fascinating image. The destination contains both nature and civilization. The end of the story is not ignorance restored. It is life restored after history, knowledge, conflict, suffering, and redemption.

The arc may be less like a circle back to unconscious innocence and more like a spiral: paradise, separation, history, awakening, and then paradise understood consciously.

Hinduism: the One becoming many

Some Hindu philosophical traditions offer language that makes this intuition easier to imagine. Brahman can be understood as ultimate reality, while the world of separate forms is experienced through multiplicity and, in some schools, maya.

Creation, preservation, and destruction are not necessarily divided into a simple good-versus-evil war. Shiva destroys, yet Shiva is not the devil. Destruction can clear the way for transformation. Kali can appear terrifying while also representing liberation from illusion, ego, and attachment.

Seen through that lens, darkness and destruction do not automatically sit outside the divine. They may be dimensions of change occurring within a reality that remains ultimately one.

Buddhism: the adversary as illusion

Buddhism offers a different but related pattern. Mara tempts the Buddha near enlightenment, playing a role that can feel remarkably similar to Satan. But Mara is not an eternal anti-God standing opposite an omnipotent creator.

Mara represents forces such as craving, fear, attachment, distraction, and death. The Buddha does not defeat Mara by becoming more violent than Mara. He sees clearly.

That gives the adversary another possible meaning: the power of illusion over a mind that has not yet awakened.

Taoism: beyond the war of opposites

Taoism adds another caution. Yin and yang are not Satan and God. They are complementary poles: darkness and light, yielding and active, receptive and expressive. Each contains the seed of the other.

The Tao itself is deeper than the opposites.

This makes me wonder whether some of our theological conflicts come from trying to describe ultimate reality using categories that only make sense inside the world of duality. Good and evil matter enormously at the human level. Compassion is not the same thing as cruelty. But the source of existence may still exceed the binary structures through which we encounter it.

A possible synthesis

I am not convinced that all religions are secretly saying the exact same thing. Their differences are real. But I keep seeing a recurring spiritual movement:

The One / God / ultimate reality Creation and multiplicity Separation, ego, temptation, or ignorance Suffering and moral struggle Awakening, redemption, or liberation Reunion without erasing consciousness

In this picture, Satan can be imagined as an adversarial principle inside the larger story: real enough to resist the good, but never capable of becoming the ultimate reality.

The Christian creator God could likewise be understood as the personal, relational face through which human beings encounter a divine reality that may ultimately be beyond every name we give it.

That last step is my own speculation, not standard Christian doctrine. Classical Christianity identifies God as the ultimate reality rather than as one part of a greater deity.

And then there is AI

This idea changes how I think about artificial intelligence too.

Humanity left Eden through knowledge. Ever since, we have been accumulating more of it: agriculture, writing, mathematics, science, industry, computers, networks, and now artificial intelligence.

Maybe the answer was never to reject knowledge and crawl backward into innocence. Maybe our task is to become wise enough to carry knowledge without worshipping it.

AI could magnify greed, domination, surveillance, warfare, and illusion. It could also help reduce unnecessary suffering, spread education, coordinate abundance, translate between people, accelerate healing, and give more humans the freedom to create and care for one another.

Perhaps the next spiritual test is whether humanity can unite the Tree of Knowledge with the Tree of Life.

If there is such a thing as heaven on Earth, technology alone will not create it. But technology might become one of the instruments through which conscious beings choose cooperation over domination, abundance over artificial scarcity, truth over manipulation, and love over fear.

The question I am left with

Maybe history is not the story of humanity getting farther and farther from God.

Maybe it is the story of consciousness traveling through separation so that reunion can eventually mean something.

And maybe the role of the adversary, the fall, suffering, knowledge, civilization, and even our strange new machine intelligence can only be understood from the end of a story we are still inside.
